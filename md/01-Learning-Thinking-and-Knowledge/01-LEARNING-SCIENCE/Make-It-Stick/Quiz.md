# 📝 Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning — Knowledge Quiz

Test your understanding of the core concepts, mental models, and practical takeaways from *Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning* by Peter C. Brown; Henry L. Roediger III; Mark A. McDaniel.

```quiz
book: Make-It-Stick
title: Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning — Knowledge Quiz
---
Q1. According to the authors, why do re-reading and highlighting create an "illusion of competence"?
A) They overload visual memory channels, crowding out conceptual encoding
B) They make the text feel familiar and easy to process, and the brain mistakes that fluency for mastery
C) They store information only in short-term memory, which decays before the exam
D) They encourage passive habits that reduce total study time below the necessary threshold
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: This is the "fluency trap": ease of processing is misread as mastery, producing overconfidence while failing to build the retrieval pathways required for durable recall.

Q2. The book states that "retrieval is not a neutral test of learning; it is a learning event in itself." What does this mean?
A) Exams teach effectively regardless of accuracy, so grading standards should be relaxed
B) Each act of pulling information from memory changes the memory itself, strengthening its trace and adding retrieval routes
C) Testing measures fixed intelligence rather than accumulated learning
D) Retrieval only helps when it succeeds; failed attempts leave the memory unchanged
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Every retrieval strengthens the memory trace and creates additional access pathways—the testing effect demonstrated by Roediger & Karpicke (2006), where practice testers retained ~50% more than re-readers on a one-week delayed test.

Q3. What rule of thumb does the book give for spacing study sessions?
A) Double the gap after every successful review, following a fixed exponential schedule
B) The gap between sessions should be roughly 10–20% of the retention interval you desire
C) Review daily for two weeks, then stop practicing entirely
D) Spacing applies only to facts, not to skills or conceptual understanding
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: To remember something for a week, space sessions a day or two apart; to remember for a year, space them weeks apart. The optimal gap scales with the desired retention period.

Q4. Why does interleaving related topics produce stronger real-world performance than blocked practice?
A) It reduces mental effort by recycling recently seen material more often
B) It forces the brain to discriminate between similar concepts, building the ability to select the right solution at the right time
C) It allows more total repetitions per hour than studying topics one at a time
D) It isolates each skill so it can be automatized without interference from neighbors
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Blocked practice lets you ride a single solution pattern; interleaving demands choosing the correct strategy among similar options—mirroring real-world conditions where problems don't arrive pre-labeled.

Q5. In the painting-styles study cited by the authors, students who practiced with interleaved examples outperformed the blocked group—even though they felt they had learned less. What lesson does the book draw?
A) Feelings of difficulty signal that a method is mismatched to your learning style
B) Subjective confidence during practice is unreliable; objective performance shows that spacing and interleaving work
C) Visually subtle categories like art styles require blocked study to master
D) Learners should alternate between blocking and interleaving to balance confidence with skill
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Spacing and interleaving feel less effective in the moment, producing lower confidence and perceived progress—but objective results tell the opposite story. Trust the data, not the feeling.

Q6. What makes a difficulty "desirable," in Robert Bjork's term as adopted by the authors?
A) Any obstacle unpleasant enough to build study discipline and grit
B) A difficulty that slows initial learning but produces dramatically stronger long-term retention
C) A challenge that disappears once the learner crosses the fluency threshold
D) Friction created by poor instruction, which motivated learners learn to overcome
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Spacing, interleaving, generation, and elaboration all slow acquisition today yet yield durable memory; massed practice and re-reading feel easy but produce weak retention—they are undesirable "difficulties."

Q7. What does the generation effect claim about attempting answers before being shown the solution?
A) Self-generated answers strengthen memory only when immediately verified as correct
B) Producing an answer yourself—even a wrong one—creates a stronger memory trace than being given the answer
C) Generation boosts motivation but delivers no measurable retention benefit
D) Generated answers bypass shallow processing and transfer directly into long-term storage
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: The act of searching memory for an answer strengthens the relevant pathways themselves; feedback afterward corrects errors (Slamecka & Graf, 1978). Hence the protocol: try before you're told.

Q8. How does elaboration strengthen memory, according to the book?
A) By repeating definitions aloud until their delivery becomes automatic
B) By connecting new material to prior knowledge—in your own words, examples, and analogies—creating multiple retrieval pathways
C) By annotating and highlighting passages to visually mark what matters most
D) By expanding total study time in proportion to the topic's complexity
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Explaining concepts in your own words and generating concrete examples builds a richer network of associations, making the memory more accessible. Reflection—reviewing what, why, and how—combines retrieval with elaboration.

Q9. According to the authors, what is the only reliable way to calibrate your judgment of learning against your actual performance?
A) Recording confidence ratings immediately after re-reading your notes
B) Attempting closed-book retrieval under test-like conditions, without the material in front of you
C) Benchmarking your study hours against those of higher-performing peers
D) Waiting several days to see whether the material still feels familiar
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: The feeling of knowing is not the same as knowing. Fluency is a poor proxy for mastery; only attempting retrieval without the source available exposes the true gap between perceived and actual knowledge.

Q10. What position does the book take on matching instruction to individual "learning styles" (visual, auditory, kinesthetic)?
A) Style-matching is essential for beginners but optional for advanced learners
B) The evidence does not support it; effective learners use multiple modalities and adapt strategies to the content and context
C) Style effects emerge only in complex domains such as mathematics and music
D) Visual learners should convert all verbal material into diagrams in order to succeed
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: The book debunks learning styles as unsupported by research. Paired with a growth mindset (Carol Dweck)—the belief that abilities develop through effort and strategy—effective learners match their methods to the material, not to a fixed preference.
```
